Module 0 · The one idea

You’re not selling software. You’re selling a festival its own map, its own fans, and its own money back.

Every festival already pays for these things — badly. They rent a ticketer that takes ~27% in fees and keeps the fan data. They pay a designer $1,000–$3,000 for a paper map. They lose the crowd the second the gates open.

StageFill gives them less fees, their data, a live map, and a growing menu of things to add — and we publish our prices when every competitor hides theirs. That transparency is the wedge.

“Land the map. Demo the value. Sell the ladder.” — the whole motion in six words.
Module 1 · Who you sell to

It’s always the same shape — a container with an owner.

One engine, four buyers. You’re looking for the person who owns the footprint and answers for it:

🎪 Festival organizer🏘 Downtown / BID (GEDP)🥕 Farmers market manager🍺 Venue owner

They differ in label — “admitted,” “bought a loaf,” “checked in” — but the tool is identical. If you can build one, you can sell all four. Start with the one you already have a door into.

Miguel’s door: he’s already selling GEDP / Easton — that’s a downtown corridor, the Easton Farmers’ Market, and festivals. One relationship, three containers.
Module 2 · The motion (5 steps)

Never pitch a deck. Hand them their own festival, half-built.

1
Warm text, then email. “Sent you something — check your email today.” Low pressure. (This exact move just got a reply in 30 minutes.)
2
Send the link, not a pitch. Their event, already started in The Maker — center, grounds, pavilions. They open it and see their place.
3
Let them build. The self-service wizard asks; they paste coordinates from Google Maps; it snaps. They own the build — so it’s right, and it’s theirs.
4
Show one wow. Tap 🎨 Cutesy — the illustrated map appears, snapped to their streets. This is the moment they stop evaluating and start wanting.
5
Sell the ladder. Land free/cheap. Then add modules — self-service, one line at a time. Expansion forever.
The first festival (KOTA) is free — it’s the reference, not the revenue. You give one away to win the relationship, then every festival after it pays.
Module 3 · The ++ menu (where the margin is)

The base gets you in. The ++ items are the business.

🎨 Cutesy Illustrated Map — $499

One-time, per festival. Generated from their geo, auto-snapped, done-for-you. Festivals pay a designer $1,000–$3,000 for a worse, un-georeferenced one.

~100% margin. TAM on this SKU alone: 800 festivals × $499 ≈ $400,000. The killer line: “$499 covers the AI cost of your custom festival map — instant, and it lines up with the real streets.”

Sell it as the wow of the demo. Escalate gently as the event date nears: “upload your own, or Buy Now $499.”

🎤 The Crowd-Games Machine — recurring · every stage · every genre

Re-imagined karaoke — really a real-time crowd-games engine: karaoke, live trivia, and genre games that match the room — Latin-music trivia at a Latin fest, Dead trivia at a jam fest, country at a country fest. The whole crowd plays on their phones together. It runs itself — and it books like an act: it drops into the lineup exactly like a band, so slotting it into a changeover or a slow Tuesday is one tap.

The festival play (the big one): fill the changeover — every stage’s 20–30 dead minutes between bands while crews set up and tear down. Instead of the crowd drifting, they’re playing: sing-alongs, trivia for prizes, a stage-wide game. Turns the festival’s worst dead air into its own little party.

Every festival, every stage, every genre. Price it at $99 an intermission set — and it pays for itself: an engaged crowd doesn’t drift to the parking lot, they stay and keep buying beers at the bar right through the changeover. The $99 comes back many times over in drink sales. One festival = many stages = many intermissions — it multiplies. (Also the venue play: a bar books a set to fill a slow night, no band guarantee.)

📊 Data + Reports (+ the sponsor gateway) — the whale

Live per-area footfall — how many people, where, when. Ops and public safety both want it. Each stakeholder (police, EMS, city, sponsors) gets their own report, so each is bought in.

The reports justify a fee — but the real number is the sponsor gateway: hard attendance data turns “estimates” into “6 gateway sponsors × $9,999,” ≈ ~$260K / festival. The data fee is the wedge; the sponsorship book is the prize.

🎫 Secure ticketing — the crown, earned last

The rotating, un-screenshottable ticket. Don’t lead with it — it’s the biggest ask and the incumbent’s only moat. Land the map + reports first; ticketing is the prize you earn once they trust you.

Module 4 · Objections & closes

Every “no” is a “not yet” with a reason. Name it first.

“We already use [Eventbrite / Events.com].”

“Great — keep them for now. Start with the map, which they don’t give you, and keep your fan data, which they take. Add the rest when you’re ready.” Land small.

“What does it cost?”

Tell them a number — instantly. Everyone else hides pricing; you don’t. “$499 for the map, and modules you turn on yourself.” The number builds trust; the vagueness is the competitor’s tell.

“I need to think about it.”

“Totally — here’s your festival already half-built; play with it tonight, nothing to sign.” The build in their hands does the closing.

“Does it actually work?”

Hand them the live one. It’s fixed — it no longer freezes when the screen sleeps. Let them feel it work on their own phone. (That gut-check is what a demo lives or dies on.)

Module 5 · Your first week

Do these five things.

  • Pick one container you already have a door into. (Miguel: GEDP / Easton.)
  • Build their thing in The Maker yourself, halfway. Center + grounds + a container or two. Fifteen minutes.
  • Warm text → email → the link. Not a deck. Their place, on their phone.
  • On the call, tap 🎨 Cutesy. That’s the wow. Watch their face.
  • Name one ++ item and one price. Usually the $499 map. Publish the number; let them say yes to something small.
Give the first one away. Win the relationship. Then the 800 are line items — and you already know how to run every one of them.
